Many business owners start with a simple request: "We need a logo." Months later, they realize the logo alone hasn't given their business a consistent presence. Their social posts look one way, their website another, and their invoices a third.

The problem isn't the logo. It's confusing three different things: **brand identity**, **user interface (UI) design**, and **user experience (UX)**.

## Brand Identity: How People Recognize You

An identity is a complete system, and the logo is only one part of it:

- **The logo** in its different versions: full, compact, and for light and dark backgrounds.
- **Colors:** A primary color, supporting colors, and when to use each.
- **Typography:** For headings and body text, in both Arabic and English.
- **Visual elements:** Shapes, icons, and photography style.
- **Tone of voice:** Formal or friendly? Concise or explanatory?

A good identity is **practical to apply**: any designer who works with you later can produce something that looks like you, without guesswork.

> At Qawsan, for example, the brackets `{ }` aren't just a logo. They're an element that recurs across our website, headings, and social posts, so they've become a mark people recognize even before they read the name.

## UI: How the Product Looks

User interface (UI) design applies the identity to screens: buttons, menus, forms, cards, and tables. A good interface is:

- **Consistent:** The primary button looks the same everywhere.
- **Clear in its hierarchy:** The eye knows where to look first.
- **Comfortable to read:** Enough contrast, deliberate spacing, and a typeface that works well for Arabic.

## UX: How It Feels to Use

User experience (UX) goes deeper than looks. It answers one question: can users get done what they came for, easily and without confusion?

An interface can be beautiful while the experience is frustrating: a sign-up form that asks for twenty fields, a booking button buried at the bottom of the page, or an error message that doesn't explain what went wrong.

Typical UX questions include:

1. What's the shortest path to completing the core task?
2. What happens when the user makes a mistake? Do they understand what to do next?
3. Does the experience hold up on mobile — one-handed, in bright sunlight?
4. Can everyone use it, including people with disabilities?

## Why You Need All Three

| Element | The Question It Answers | When It's Missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | Who are you? | You look like every competitor |
| UI | How do you look? | A cluttered or inconsistent product |
| UX | Does it work for the user? | Visitors leave before they buy or book |

Identity captures attention and builds trust, the interface translates that identity into screens, and the experience turns a visitor into a user who comes back.

## What Makes Arabic Design Different

Designing for Arabic-speaking users isn't just "flipping" an English design from right to left. A few details make all the difference:

- Choosing an Arabic typeface that stays legible on small screens and comes in multiple weights.
- Directional icons, like arrows, need to be mirrored, while others, like the play icon, don't.
- Mixed text (Arabic alongside English names or numbers) needs careful handling so the word order doesn't break.
- Arabic text runs to different lengths than English, and the design has to accommodate that.

## The Bottom Line

A beautiful logo is a start, but it isn't an identity — and a beautiful identity isn't enough if the experience is confusing. When identity, interface, and experience are built as one system, your product becomes easy to recognize and easy to use.
